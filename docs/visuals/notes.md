# What the plots show

No matplotlib, so everything here is text, generated by `python src/visualise.py` from the
checkpoint of the first Shakespeare run.

A picture on its own proves nothing, so each one gets a paragraph on what I think it means.

## Loss curve

Full version in [loss-curve.txt](loss-curve.txt). `t` is training loss, `v` is validation.

```
  5.02 | t
  4.54 | v
  3.73 | t t
  3.40 | v vt v
  2.92 |       tttv  v     v
  2.59 |           vttt vvt tvtvt  t    v t   v  t  v   tt t     t      v
  2.43 |             tvt   t t tvttv ttvtt  ttt tvtvttttv vt  vtvv  vttt tt vt
  2.27 |                t        v  t   tvvv   t   ttv v vtvttt ttvtt  tt vttvt
       +----------------------------------------------------------------------
        1                                                                 2000
```

Three phases, and they match the three things the model can learn in that order.

Steps 1 to about 100, a cliff from 5.02 down to around 3.4. This is not the model getting clever. It
is learning that characters have different base rates. Space is 15.23% of the text and `e` is 8.48%,
so guessing in proportion already beats guessing uniformly. Cheap and fast, which is why the drop is
so steep. Worth knowing so I don't read this part as intelligence.

Steps 100 to about 600, down to around 2.5. Now it is using the previous character or two. Which
letters follow which, a capital after a newline.

Steps 600 to 2000, a slow noisy grind from 2.5 to 2.2. This is where real spelling would have to be
learned, and 29,697 parameters with a 32 character context is not much to do it with. It was still
improving when I stopped it.

The training line is noisy because each point is a single batch of 4 sequences, so it is one small
sample rather than a measurement of the whole corpus. Validation sits at or below training the whole
way, so this is underfitting rather than overfitting. The fix is a bigger model or a longer run, not
regularisation.

## Attention grids

Full version in [attention-heads.txt](attention-heads.txt). Each grid is one head, cell `(i, j)` is
how much position `i` attended to position `j`, shaded against each row's own maximum. Blank above
the diagonal is the mask, and those cells are exactly zero.

Shaded per row rather than against 1.0 on purpose: a row sums to 1 spread over `i+1` cells, so later
rows are diluted and would all look blank on an absolute scale.

Block 0, head 0 is a previous character head. Almost all of the weight sits on the diagonal and the
cell to its left:

```
        F i r s t _ C i t i z e n :
t      |      ++@@
_      |        --@@
C      |          @@++
i      |            @@
t      |          ....@@##
```

The `t` looks at itself and a little at the `s` before it. Local, bigram-ish, and it is what a model
needs first: what just happened.

Block 1, head 1 does something different. It goes back to structural anchors instead of neighbours:

```
:      |++                        @@
\n     |..                        @@
w      |          %%                              @@..
_      |          ##  ::                          @@    ##
g      |          **  ..                          @@    %%
```

Several positions late in the sentence attend back to the start of the line and to column 10, rather
than to whatever came immediately before them. So the second block is not repeating the first one's
job. It is using the room that block 0's local head freed up to work out where it is in the line,
and that is what produces the `NAME:` newline pattern in the samples.

Local first, then structural. That progression is why two layers beat one here.

The honest bit: one head in each block has a clear job and the other looks scattered. I don't know
whether that is a small model failing to specialise both heads or a real pattern, and it is in
questions.md as an open one.

## Embedding neighbours

Full version in [embedding-neighbours.md](embedding-neighbours.md). Cosine similarity between rows
of the embedding table, so 1.0 means the same direction and 0.0 means unrelated.

```
. -> ? 0.96, ! 0.92, ; 0.73, 3 0.67, : 0.64, , 0.62
t -> T 0.66, w 0.35, W 0.35, s 0.33, g 0.33, y 0.32
a -> i 0.59, A 0.55, u 0.51, O 0.50, o 0.44, b 0.38
o -> O 0.50, q 0.47, i 0.45, a 0.44, I 0.34, j 0.29
q -> Q 0.55, B 0.53, J 0.53, o 0.47, g 0.43, X 0.41
_ -> - 0.75, & 0.52, \n 0.46, X 0.45, A 0.34, i 0.29
```

Nothing in my code groups characters. They are just ids into a table of random numbers at the start.
Any structure here was learned from which characters turned out to be useful in the same places.

Sentence ending punctuation has collapsed into one cluster. `.` and `?` are at 0.96 and `!` at 0.92,
which is very nearly the same vector. That makes sense: for the purpose of predicting what comes
next, those three are interchangeable, because all of them are followed by a space or a newline and
then a capital. The model found that out on its own.

Upper and lower case versions of the same letter have paired up. `t` with `T` at 0.66, `a` with `A`
at 0.55, `o` with `O` at 0.50, `q` with `Q` at 0.55. Nothing connects them in the tokenizer, they are
just two unrelated ids. They are close because they behave the same way in text.

Whitespace groups with whitespace, space and newline at 0.46.

Vowels are grouping a bit too, `a` is closest to `i`, `u`, `o`, but weakly. That would probably
sharpen with a wider `d_model` or a longer run.

What I am not going to claim: `z -> v 0.80` and `q -> B 0.53` are hard to justify. My guess is that
rare characters never get enough gradient to be pushed anywhere meaningful, so they keep most of
their random start and any similarity between two of them is noise. Rare characters staying random is
the expected outcome, and it is better to say that than to invent a story for it.
